## Cheap Hands and Angry Ones

*“Within the decade came power that did not depend on fuel markets, machines that could raise a district from raw material in a season, and robots able to do almost any manual task. Each of these threw someone out of work or into debt. The builders met the new machines first with disbelief and then with orders. The labor disputes of the late 2060s are among the best documented events of the age. The strikers were furious about losing their jobs, exactly as people always have been, and the fact that the thing taking their jobs was a robot from nowhere hardly seemed to trouble them.”*

— *The Road to the Stars*

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A3 · Marek Kowalczyk*

### *Gdańsk, 2067*

His last pay slip was folded small enough to sit in a closed fist, and the rent figure on it was circled twice in blue pen. Marek Kowalczyk had circled it himself on the Friday they let him go, standing in the yard office with the foreman looking away. Three weeks later the circle had worn through the paper at one corner, and he could feel the hole with his thumb through the lining of his jacket.

He was fifty-two years old, and he had been a welder at the Northern Yard since he was nineteen. His hands knew a seam the way other men's hands knew the faces of their children. He could lay a bead on a curved hull plate in a crosswind with the arc held steady at the width of a pencil lead, and for thirty-three years that had been enough to buy a flat, a pension plan, and a daughter's place at the technical college. The flat was rented now. The plan had a letter attached to it explaining why it was worth less than it had been. The daughter did not know yet that he had stopped sending the fees.

He had watched the crew arrive in the spring. They came on long flat trucks with no company name on the doors, and they unfolded in the old rail yard behind the harbor like patient insects, a hundred or so jointed gray frames moving on legs, with arms that ended in tools he had no word for. Nobody shouted orders at them. A man in a hard hat stood at the edge of the site holding a tablet and a mug of coffee, and that was the whole of the supervision.

In six weeks, a district. Not a building, a district, with towers and walkways and a covered market, and a long clean quay on the water where the shipyard's east berth used to be. He had stood at the fence one lunch hour and watched a robot lay a seam along a hull-grade beam, and he had seen at once that it was better than his. Straighter, and cooler, and without a flicker of hesitation at the join. He had no quarrel with a seam.

His quarrel was with the Friday.

That was the thing he could not get the younger men on the line to understand, the ones who shouted about machines and wanted to burn them. The robots had not come to his door and taken anything. Somebody had signed a paper, and somebody else had stamped it, and a man in a pressed shirt had said the word *restructuring* to a room of forty welders as if it were weather. The robot was a tool. Nobody blamed the hammer. He wanted the hand that picked it up, and the hand had a pension, and a lawyer.

Now he walked in the march with a cardboard sign cut from a washing-machine box, the black marker already smeared by the drizzle. PAY THE RENT, it said, because that was the only sentence he had left. The wooden stake it was nailed to had gone cold and slick, and his palm had gone numb around it half a mile ago.

There were thousands of them. Welders and crane drivers, dockers, a block of fitters from the pipe works with their union banner heavy in the wet, and women from the canteen who had been laid off the same week as the men. A drummer somewhere ahead was keeping a slow beat, and the whole crowd was walking to it without being asked. The police walked along both flanks in dark rain gear, and none of them looked at anyone. The march was bound for the Eastgate works, where the robot crews were housed, and where, a man behind him said, a woman in a firm's jacket was holding the gate.

The man beside him offered a cigarette. Marek shook his head. He did not trust his fingers to hold it.

"They'll listen this time," the man said. "There are too many of us."

Marek nodded. He wanted to believe it. A lot of people wanted to believe it, which was why they had come, and why the drummer kept the beat, and why the whole street felt for a moment like a single large animal breathing.

He glanced back to see how far the column ran.

Three rows behind him, a man in a green coat had his right hand buried in the front of it.

The coat hung wrong.

Something square and dark pressed against the cloth.

Steel.

His grip loosened on the stake.

The sign tilted. The rain ran down the cardboard.

His hand went slack. The sign slid out of it and hit the road.

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A3 · Hannah Shepard (36)*

### *The Eastgate works, 2067*

There were no police at the gate. Hannah Shepard had asked for them twice, in writing, and the answer had come back both times in the same polite sentence: the permit line was two blocks east, and the city did not intend to cross it. So the gate belonged to her firm. Thirty-one people in gray jackets stood across a steel apron the width of a road, shields grounded, and behind them the robot crews waited in the yard like furniture under dust sheets.

She was thirty-six, and she had learned to read a line by its gaps. The gaps here were wrong. A demonstration this size drew marshals, a medical tent, a city liaison with a radio and a lanyard, and today the road held none of them. Only the strikers were coming, thousands of them, under a gray drizzle that blurred their signs into one slow-moving smear of cardboard.

Her orders to the line were short. Hold. No weapons drawn. Let them shout. She had walked the line twice that morning, adjusting a strap here, a stance there, and every face under the visors was a face she had hired and knew by name.

She also knew the faces coming up the road, or ones very like. A shipwright's forearms, a docker's stoop, a crane driver's squint. Her father had worn that squint for forty years. She was not here to fight them, only to keep a gate shut, and she held onto the difference the way she held the shield.

Near the back of the column, in the corner of her eye, two men in union vests hauled a third man out of the crowd and down a side street. He wore a green coat. He did not resist, and neither of them said a word. Whatever that was, she noted it and let it go.

The drummer's beat reached the apron and stopped.

For a long moment the road was silent. A single voice at the front said, quite quietly, "Open the gate."

"No," Hannah said. "Not today."

The crowd leaned.

She felt it before she saw it. A pressure along the line, like a tide turning, the weight of three thousand people who had walked a long way and had nowhere left to put it. The front rank hit her shields.

"Hold!"

Her boots slid on the wet. She drove her shoulder into the plastic. The shield shuddered against her forearm.

Voices. A scream, thin and high. Bodies pressing in from the rear and pushing the front ranks onto the line.

Her left hand found the officer beside her, Okafor, a big quiet man she had hired three months earlier, and braced his belt. "Steady."

"They're not hitting us," he shouted.

"Don't give them a reason."

The crowd surged again. Someone fell. Hands reached down, dragged him up, and the press closed over the gap.

She counted nothing. She watched the front ranks, the open mouths, the raised palms. Nobody held a weapon. A woman with a canteen apron had both hands flat against Hannah's shield, her face a foot from the visor, her lips shaping words Hannah could not hear.

Then a thing arced out of the gray over the heads of the crowd.

It was small. Dark. It turned once in the air.

It struck Okafor's visor with a crack.

He went to one knee.

Blood ran from under the rim.

Hannah's arm locked on the shield. She did not look at him.

She looked up.

The stone had not come from the strikers. They were all in front of her, hands empty, faces turned toward the gate. It had come from behind them, from the roof of the warehouse across the road, over three thousand heads.

A figure stood on the edge of the roof.

It stepped back out of sight.

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A3 · Maya Brooks (30)*

### *Brussels, 2068*

The requisition had come back with the approval line empty. Maya Brooks laid it flat on the blotter and ran her thumb along the rule where an initial belonged, as though the ink might surface if she warmed the paper. She had drafted the thing herself two days earlier, in the plain gray register of a legal-aid foundation, and she had sent it up the usual chain with the usual cover sheet. The chain had returned it untouched, and clipped to its corner was a single slip of cream paper she had not put there.

Outside her window a tram rattled past the Rue de la Loi, and the afternoon smelled of wet slate and roasting chestnuts. It had been three days since the Eastgate march, and the story had already settled into its shape on the front pages: a mob, a gate, a stone, a guard in hospital. Nobody had written the other half of it, which was that three thousand people had walked for two hours in the rain without breaking a single window until something came over their heads.

She had worked the numbers on the morning train, because she worked numbers the way other people said grace. Sixty-one arrests at the gate, of whom forty had no record at all. Fourteen injured, among them one officer from the security firm that had held the line, a firm she knew by the name on the incident report and the name of the woman who ran it. Legal fees at the going rate for the first month came to a figure she wrote down twice to be sure of.

That was the first half of the job. The second half was quieter. Three legislators in two parliaments were drafting retraining acts, bills that offered displaced workers a stipend and a course and a promise. They were decent men, tired and underfunded, and they would write better bills with a little help, a research grant here, a campaign contribution there, a foundation willing to host the hearings. She had a list. She had the sums beside each name.

It was Influence work, the kind she did well. She had stopped telling herself it was only arithmetic around the time she turned twenty-eight. What she told herself instead was that someone had to hold the strikers up, because without a lawyer they would be ground down in a month, and without a bill their anger had nowhere to go but the street. It was, by any honest reading, a kindness. It was also exactly the shape of the thing she had been trained to see in other people's governments, a hand that funded both the protest and the remedy.

And the stone was not in her ledger.

She had checked the Action desk's log that morning before coffee. Nothing was tasked for Eastgate, and nothing was scheduled for the roofs across the ring road. She had pulled the file and read it twice, and the dates simply did not touch. She did not ask what that meant. She had learned that the right question, asked too early, sometimes closed a door that would have opened by itself.

A man had stood on a roof. Somebody had put him there, and it was not anyone she knew.

She turned back to the slip of cream paper clipped to the requisition's corner. It was a plain half-sheet, torn clean along one edge. She had avoided it for ten minutes, the way one avoids a letter that might be a summons.

She unclipped it.

Three lines.

*Fund their defense.*

*Fund the ones who will write the acts.*

*Let them be angry. Keep them alive.*

There was no signature.

She did not need one. The sevens were crossed in the continental way, and the capitals leaned a few degrees too far right, as though the writer had spent his life writing in a hurry, in the dark. She had seen that hand on one other document, in a ward in Marseille, six years ago, under the word *approved*.

She set the page down on the blotter.

She squared its edges against the desk.

She took her hand away very slowly, as if the paper might move.

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A3 · Aurelio Benedetti*

### *Turin, 2068*

The drawing lay half finished on the site table, held flat at the corners by a thermos and three bolts. Aurelio Benedetti had begun it at six that morning, a stair elevation for the hospital wing on the north block, in the soft 2B pencil he had carried in his shirt pocket for forty years. The stringers were done and the treads were done. The handrail, which was the only part of a stair that anybody ever touched, was still a pale suggestion in the margin.

It was Thursday. His crew's last paid shift ended at six on Friday, and by then he had to have walked the machines through everything he knew.

That was the order, and it had come on a printed sheet, not a call. *Complete the handover of site practice to the automated crew by close of shift Friday. Foremen will remain on payroll until handover is confirmed.* He had read the last sentence a dozen times and kept finding the same meaning in it. When the handover was confirmed, he stopped being on the payroll. The only way to keep his wages was to go slowly, and the only way to keep his self-respect was to go well.

He had been a builder since he was seventeen. His father had laid brick in this city after the war, and his grandfather before that, and Aurelio had grown up knowing that a wall was a sum of small decisions made by someone who had cared. He had met the machines in March with plain disbelief. Forty-three of them had stepped off the trucks, gray and jointed, with a long tidy movement that reminded him of herons in a rice field, and he had laughed. A man could be forgiven for laughing. Nothing that moved like that could lay a course of stone.

By April they had laid the first course. By May they had finished the shell of the block, and he had stood beneath it in the dusk with his hands hanging empty, because it was straight, and square, and better than anything his crew had built in years.

Disbelief had lasted six weeks. After it came orders.

Now one of the machines stood a pace behind his left shoulder, silent, patient as a tree. It was taller than he was, and its housing gave off a dry, even warmth he could feel on the side of his neck, with a faint smell like a toaster left on. It had been shadowing him since eight. It did not ask questions. It watched his hand.

He had tried, in the first days, to talk to it, the way one talks to a new apprentice. He had explained why old stone wanted a wet mortar and old brick a dry one. He had said, *you feel it, in the trowel,* and the machine had done nothing visible at all, and the next morning it had pointed a manipulator at a joint his own men had missed. He had stopped talking after that. It was not rude. It was that a man could not teach a thing that had already learned.

But he went on drawing. It was the one part of the job that still felt like his.

The pencil lead whispered across the paper. He added the first baluster, the second, the long clean line of the rail swelling toward the newel post. His lower back ached. A chill came off the bare concrete, and the light through the unglazed window had gone the color of dishwater. He thought of his crew, waiting for him in the canteen, thirty-one men and women with their lunch boxes closed and their eyes on the door. He thought of the question none of them had asked yet, which was what they would do on Monday.

He had reached the point on the drawing where the rail met the newel, the join that took a joiner's whole attention, and where a good stair was separated from a stair that merely stood.

His pencil stopped.

Over his shoulder, the machine moved.

It stepped past him. It lowered an arm. A slender instrument unfolded from its wrist.

The point touched the paper.

A line drew itself, fine and quick, from the end of his rail to the post.

Then another. The mortise. The cap. The small returned scroll at the foot that his own father had taught him to draw, and that nobody had ever told the machine.

Eleven seconds.

Aurelio stood very still.

The pencil in his hand began to shake. He could not make it stop.

The drawing was finished.

It was exactly the line he had been about to draw.

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A3 · Hannah Shepard (38)*

### *Gdańsk, 2069*

The service plate on the robot's forearm had three lines stamped into the steel. *Model.* *Serial.* *Owner.* The first two were filled in with a clean, shallow pressing, and the third was bare metal, polished by handling to a dull shine, as though a hundred thumbs had gone looking for a name there and found none.

Hannah Shepard had stood in front of that plate for a week of mornings. She was thirty-eight years old, and her firm had taken on thirty-four new hires in the spring, all of them laid off that winter from the Northern Yard or the pipe works or the cranes. She trained them in the open lot behind the Eastgate works, a cracked apron of asphalt with a view of the harbor and the long clean towers of the new district rising beyond it, and she trained them with a robot standing idle at the edge of the lot, because that was the thing they would be guarding.

She had not expected the idea to work. Her board had called it sentiment, and her accountant had called it exposure. But she had spent a month after the gate looking at the faces on the other side of her shield, and she had come to think that anger was only energy that nobody had given a job. These people knew the sites. They knew every crane and every seam, and they noticed, with a craftsman's sourness, when something was done wrong. A man who had once welded a hull plate could tell at forty paces whether a gate had been fitted by someone who cared. Put him on a perimeter, and he would never let the gate rest.

It had worked better than she had hoped. Four of them had already caught faults her old patrols missed, and one, a woman from the canteen, had talked down a drunk contractor at midnight with a plain, level courtesy that Hannah could not have taught.

Okafor stood by the water cooler with the new line of white scar over his eyebrow, which he wore, she suspected, as a kind of silent credential. He had come back in six weeks. Nobody had asked him to.

The lot smelled of damp tarmac and diesel and the sea. A gull stood on the robot's shoulder, and the robot did not move. She laid her hand on the plate and let her thumb cross the blank, as she had every morning. The metal was cold and slightly gritty, and it told her nothing.

She had asked, naturally. Her contract with the district's developer ran through a holding company, and the holding company answered to another, and the other was registered at an address in a city she had never visited, with a lawyer's name beneath it and no one's beneath that. She had written to the lawyer. The reply had come in four days, courteous and complete, and it had said exactly nothing. She had asked her husband, and he had said, in his steady voice, that it was a matter for the registrars. She had looked at him a moment longer than she needed to. He had looked at his tea.

She did not like what she was noticing. She had spent most of her life learning to read a room by what was absent, and the thing absent here was not small. A fleet of machines the size of an army had raised an entire district in a season, and there was no name on it.

"Captain."

She turned.

The man had come up behind her without a sound, which was a thing the old yard hands could do. He was in his fifties, broad in the shoulder, with his hands hanging easy at his sides and a cold wet look at the corner of his eye. She knew his face from the first day of the course. He had told her his name, and she had forgotten it, and she remembered now that it was Marek. He had been quiet. He had watched everything with the flat patient attention of someone waiting for a verdict.

"Marek," she said.

"I was at Eastgate. In the march." He did not say it as a challenge. He said it as a fact he had been carrying and wished to set down. "Three rows from the front."

"Then you saw the stone."

"I saw the man in the green coat first." He glanced at the robot's plate, then at her. "Captain, I'll ask you something. You can tell me to go to hell."

"Ask."

"Who owns them?"

The gull shifted on the robot's shoulder.

Okafor, at the cooler, had gone still.

She could have said a dozen things. She could have named the developer, the holding company, the lawyer in the distant city. She had all the answers that were true on paper.

Her thumb was still on the blank.

"I don't know," Hannah said. "Not all of it."
