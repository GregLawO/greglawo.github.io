# Those Who Walked Out Whole

*“The clinics were built around a single apparatus, the Med-Pod. Patients entered it ill or broken and, by their own accounts, left restored. The accounts do not gush. They are written in a stunned, flat register, like those of survivors of an accident who have not yet decided whether to believe they are alive. Patients crossed borders to reach the clinics. Families waited to see a cure work on a neighbor before trusting it with their own. By the 2060s the clinics stood in all the larger economies, though standing everywhere is a different matter from being accepted.”*

— *The Road to the Stars*

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A1 · Hannah Shepard (22)*

### *Galveston, 2053*

The wall behind the front desk was bare paint, exactly where a license should have hung, and Hannah Shepard had been staring at it since before dawn. The Galveston clinic smelled of antiseptic laid over Gulf humidity, a clean sting on top of salt and wet concrete, and somewhere behind the walls a ventilation fan ticked like a cooling engine. Her dress blues were folded over the chair beside her, the single gold stripe on the sleeve cuff catching the glow of a vending machine. She had worn the commission for four months, and the weight of it did nothing for her now.

Her father had gone through the frosted doors three days earlier, gray as old rope, with the cancer in his pancreas outrunning every prognosis in Houston. A foreman from his old shipyard crew, whose brother had walked out of a place like this one, had written the address on a napkin, and she had followed it the way a person follows a rumor when every sensible road has run out.

She had spent the three days doing what the Navy had taught her to do, which was to notice what was missing. The staff spoke in the same flat voice, and none of them wore a name tag. She had signed a single sheet of paper at intake, with no insurance questions, no billing office, and no quoted price, and the sheet itself carried no letterhead.

A business with nothing to hide hangs its license where customers can read it. Whatever this place was, it was guarding something, and it was being very careful about what it did not say.

The frosted doors opened at ten past six, and her father walked out on his own feet. He was wearing the clothes he had gone in with, but they no longer hung on him; the gray was gone from his face, and the hollows under his eyes had filled in as if someone had simply returned what was taken. He stood straighter than she had seen him stand in a decade. He looked at her, then at the bare wall, then back at her, and said in his ordinary voice, the one from every Saturday of her childhood, “Any chance of breakfast? Eggs. I could eat a horse.”

She could not answer. She crossed the room and took his wrist and held it, the way the academy had drilled her to check a pulse, because checking was the only thing her hands knew how to do, and the beat under her fingers was steady and strong and unhurried, nothing like the thready flutter she had felt in the ambulance. He let her hold on, and when she finally let go he laughed, softly, and she realized she had been crying for some time without noticing.

A woman in gray scrubs appeared at the edge of the room with a tablet held against her chest. She did not smile, and she did not offer her name. “There is also a training program,” she said, in that same voice. “Knowledge delivered directly, in a single session. Your father has been offered a place in it. You are eligible as well, if you wish.”

Hannah asked who ran the program, and the woman said, “The clinic’s operators.” Hannah asked for a name, and the woman said it again, word for word, with the patience of a recording. Hannah looked at her father. He was watching her with the look he had worn through twenty-two years of her digging in her heels over small things, the look of a man who could tell when the thing she was digging in over was large.

“No,” she said. “Thank you. No.”

The woman nodded as if she had written the answer down before the question. Her father said only, “Your call, kid,” and did not press her. It was the first time she could remember him letting a thing go without talking her around. He turned his hand over and studied his knuckles, where a welding scar she had known since childhood had vanished.

They crossed to the front door, her father a step ahead with his hand already reaching for the handle, the humidity pressing against the glass like a held breath. Outside, a gull was already working the wet street.

Paper whispered on tile.

A white card slid under the door.

She stopped. Her father did not hear it.

She bent. Picked it up.

Her fingers locked on the edge.

Blank on one side. Blank on the other. No name. No number. No seal.

She reached past his shoulder and wrenched the door open.

The step was empty.

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A1 · Lucía Ferreira*

### *A border clinic, 2058*

The slip of paper had gone soft in Lucía’s fist, the ink running at its edges, and she had not dared to look at it since the clerk pressed it into her hand. The river had been shallower than the smuggler promised and colder than anything she had imagined. She had carried Mateo across it on her hip with her skirt knotted above her knees, and she had felt his heat through two layers of wet cloth, the dry, papery heat of a body that had stopped keeping its own accounts. He was six years old.

His kidneys had failed in the spring, and the doctors in the capital had told her, kindly and without meeting her eyes, to make him comfortable.

The clinic stood on the far bank, three streets back from the water. It was a squat building of pale concrete with a green cross painted over the door, and it looked like any pharmacy or dental practice she had ever passed without a glance.

She had expected something grander, or something stranger. Instead there were plastic chairs bolted to the floor, a water cooler with an empty cup dispenser, and a television murmuring in the corner about a football match. The ordinariness unsettled her more than any marble hall would have, since a place that cured the dying ought to look as though it knew what it was.

The queue ran the length of the waiting room and out the side door into a yard, where families sat on upturned crates and folded blankets. Nobody pushed, and nobody called out. Lucía had seen crowds at hospitals before, and they had always carried a low, ragged noise of pleading. This crowd was quiet in a different way, watchful, like people at the edge of a frozen pond waiting to see whether somebody else’s weight would hold.

She learned the custom from the woman beside her, a farmer’s wife from the uplands who had been sitting there since the previous morning. “You do not simply go in,” the woman said. “You wait, and you watch.”

The woman’s neighbor’s boy had gone through the back doors on Tuesday with a growth in his throat, and the whole yard was waiting for him to come out. If he walked out whole and ate his supper like a child, the rest of them would trust the machine; if he did not, they would carry their own children home again and keep their money.

“And if he does come out?” Lucía asked.

“Then we still watch him for a week,” the woman said. “Cures that happen fast can unhappen fast.”

Over the clerk’s desk, a board said the numbers were called in order and the day’s numbers closed at sundown. Through the side door, the light in the yard had already turned the color of weak tea.

Lucía understood the woman completely. She had spent her savings on three treatments that promised everything, and she had buried her mother-in-law’s faith along with the second one. She knew what hope cost, and how little of it one person could afford. So she sat down on the floor with Mateo across her lap and the clerk’s slip in her fist, and she watched the double doors at the back of the room, because watching was the only thing she could still do for him.

A gray-haired man came out on his own feet and then wept without any sound. A girl of about twelve held a crutch she no longer seemed to need, and she carried it as if she were embarrassed by it. Each time the doors opened every head turned together, and each time the people who came out looked like survivors of a shipwreck, stunned and quiet and blinking in the strange ordinary light. Nobody cheered, and Lucía wondered whether that was because they did not yet dare to.

Mateo stirred against her collarbone and said something in his sleep. She smoothed the damp hair back from his forehead and found the skin hotter than before. She told herself the doors would open for them soon. She told herself the number in her fist was only paper, and that paper could be made to mean anything.

The double doors banged open.

A boy shot through.

Barefoot. Skinny. Laughing.

He crossed the tiles at a run. His feet slapped. He skidded past her knee.

Iker. The neighbor’s boy, the one the whole yard had been waiting on.

His mother screamed his name and caught him up off the floor.

The clerk lifted a card from the desk.

“Forty-one,” she called.

Lucía looked at the slip in her fist.

Her arms tightened around Mateo.

She opened her fingers.

Fifty-eight.

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A1 · Anselm Roque*

### *A district clinic, 2059*

The closure order rode on Anselm Roque’s knee in a cream ministry envelope, still sealed, its flap gone tacky in the damp heat of the waiting room. He had signed for it at seven that morning, and the clerk at the district office had set a rubber stamp the size of a fist in his bag beside it, as though the two belonged together. In eleven years with the licensing department he had never carried an order that weighed so little.

The file behind it was thick. Private hospitals had complained, and the pharmacists’ guild, and one insurance board that had watched its claims dry up in a single season, and every complaint used the same words: unlicensed practice, fraud against the sick. His instructions were plain enough to recite. Inspect the premises, seal the doors, post the notice, and be home before dark.

The building itself told him little. It had been a parcel depot once, and the sorting racks had left pale ladders on the far wall where the paint had been shielded from the sun. Someone had hung a child’s drawing over the worst of the damp, and someone else had propped the side door open with a bucket to let the afternoon through. Nothing here was hidden, which was the first thing he could not account for, since the frauds he had closed had all kept very good locks.

The bench under him was warm from the bodies that had sat on it before his, and he could feel the heat through his trousers. Nobody in the room was in a hurry, which was the second. People waiting at a hospital fidgeted and watched the clock, and they carried the smell of fear in their coats. These people sat with their hands in their laps, and the room smelled only of soap and wet wool.

His own father had died in a ward that cost more per night than Anselm earned in a month, and the bill had outlived him by six years. He did not think of it as a grudge. He thought of it as a figure he knew by heart, and it sat in his chest now beside the envelope.

“You’re from the government,” said the old woman next to him. She did not say it unkindly. “They all sit like that, holding the letter in both hands.”

He looked down. He was holding it in both hands.

“My brother went in last Tuesday,” she said. “The neighbors carried him on a door, the way you carry a man to a grave. Today they say he walks out.” She nodded toward the double doors at the back of the room.

The doors swung open at a quarter past four. A man came through them with a door under one arm, a plain wooden door with its hinge plates still screwed to the edge, but he carried it as if it weighed nothing. He had the look of someone who had been ill for years and had just been told it was over, a little embarrassed and mostly hungry. The old woman put her hand over her mouth.

He stopped at the end of Anselm’s bench. “Mind your feet,” he said, and turned the door sideways to pass. He smelled of soap.

Anselm drew his boots back. It was the smallest courtesy anyone had offered him in a year, and it loosened something under his ribs.

The order said fraud, but fraud needed a patient who failed later, and the file held not one. A man who had been carried in on a door was carrying it out under one arm. A career of paper had given Anselm a form for every lie that could be forged, but none for a thing he could simply watch go by.

The ministry had no such form either. It would read his report and file the closure order beside it, and he had never once learned which of the two it believed.

He slid the envelope into his bag, but he did not break the seal.

He took out the inspection form.

He uncapped his pen.

The form had a box marked *Findings*.

The box was an inch tall.

The nib scratched. His thumb whitened on the barrel.

Two words. A line under them. His name.

*Nothing found.*

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A1 · Hannah Shepard (28)*

### *Galveston, 2059*

The third lamp in the car park had been dead for eleven days, and Hannah Shepard had filed three requests to replace it. She stood at the staff door with one hand resting on the curve of her stomach, a habit she no longer noticed, and watched the dark patch where the light should have fallen across the asphalt. The night shift was ending. Behind her the corridor hummed with the ordinary noise of the Galveston complex, floor polishers and monitors and a nurse laughing at something on a tablet.

Five months. The child had learned to kick whenever she stood still, and it kicked now, twice, as if asking what she was looking at. She had resigned her commission three years ago to run security here, and the work had taught her to read a place by what it lacked.

The Thursday delivery van had not come. The stool at the gate was empty, and the guard’s coffee cup was gone with him.

Small absences, each with a reasonable excuse. She turned to call it in.

The window beside her went white.

Sound came second.

The door slammed into her. The floor rose. Her shoulder hit tile.

Glass rained down the corridor. Alarms began. Smoke rolled in at knee height.

She rolled onto her side. Her palm found her stomach. Pressed.

She got up.

Doors opened along the ward. A nurse. Two orderlies. A technician with a coffee cup still in her fist.

“East wing,” Hannah said. “Walk.”

They ran anyway. She ran behind them, herding, checking faces. One was missing. The night pharmacist.

She found him in the dispensary, flat under a fallen shelf, one hand moving. She dragged the shelf off. She hauled him up by the collar. He leaned on her. She leaned on the wall.

Low in her back, something tightened.

She kept walking.

The east doors banged open. Gulf air, wet and warm. Floodlights. A line of staff on the lawn, bent double, coughing. She ran the roster in her head against the faces. All of them. All there.

Her knees went.

A medic caught her elbow. She sat down on the grass. Heat ran down the inside of her legs. She did not look.

“Ma’am. Ma’am, lie back.”

She lay back. The sky was orange at one edge. The child was not kicking.

She waited. She waited through a count of ten, and then another.

The medic’s hands were gentle, but they stopped moving.

“I’m sorry,” he said.

They carried her in on a gurney, down the east corridor with the glass still crunching under the wheels. The ceiling lights slid past in a row. One flickered.

A door banged at the far end.

Her husband came down the corridor at a run, coatless, and slowed when he saw her. He stopped at the gurney. He took the rail in both hands. His knuckles went pale, and the rail shook, because his hands were shaking.

He looked at her face. Then at her stomach. Then at her face.

“Never again,” he said. His voice was steady, but his hands were not. “No child. Not until no one can reach you.”

The medic stood very still. The wheels ticked as the gurney settled.

“Not until then,” Hannah said.

He bent his head until his forehead touched the rail beside her hand.

Her hand lay on her stomach.

It did not move.

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A1 · Maya Brooks (24)*

### *Marseille, 2062*

The bracelet on her wrist carried a number, a barcode, and a strip of blank white plastic where a name should have been printed. Maya Brooks had read it so many times that the digits had stopped being digits. She had been dying in Marseille for fourteen months, the last three of them in rooms where nobody said the word.

She counted the exits because she could not help it. Two doors, one to the corridor and one to a utility stair; a window painted shut along its bead; a ceiling vent too narrow for a child. Eleven steps from the bed to the corridor door, which was nine more than she could walk now. Six years of reading other countries’ ministries had trained her to find the way out of a room before she found the chair, and the leukemia had taken nearly everything but that.

The man who came in at noon sat on the visitor’s chair and turned it a quarter turn, so that he could watch the door as well. She noticed. He noticed her noticing, and something in his face approved. He wore a gray coat in July and carried no flowers and no folder.

“Mademoiselle Brooks.” No one in this building had used her name in a month. “Your paper on the Ankara tariff schedules was the best thing your directorate produced last year. It was also the last, I understand, because you stopped coming in.”

“I am not receiving visitors.”

“You are receiving one.” He said it gently, with no apology in it. “There is a machine on the second floor. People go in as you are, and they come out as they were. I will not ask you to believe me. I will ask you to let it be tried.”

She had heard this speech from three different men, and each had wanted her savings. “What does it cost?”

“Nothing you are carrying.” He let a silence sit. She counted it, because counting was what she did with silences. Four seconds. A man who rehearsed his pauses was holding something back, which was not the same as lying about the machine. “Afterward, a conversation. There is work, and it is the sort you are good at.”

She looked at the window, painted shut, and at the wedge of white sky in it. She had run out of better questions.

“Yes,” she said.

Three days later, the frosted doors opened.

She had expected the walk to hurt, but it did not. Eleven steps to the corridor door, and her legs took them. She did not need the wall. She did not need the rail.

Twelve. Thirteen.

She kept going.

Her right hand was tapping against her thigh. Two doors. One stair. One sealed window. She could not make it stop.

The corridor ran thirty paces to a glass door. Beyond the glass, a car park and the sun. She counted the paces. She counted the fire exits, and the cameras, and the man at the end.

Gray coat. Hands in pockets. He had turned his chair into a stance, and his back was to the glass so that he could see her come.

She stopped three paces short. Her heart was loud in her ears, strong and slow, a beat she had not heard in a year.

He smiled, a little. Not with his teeth.

“Sixty more years, for a start,” he said. “Mademoiselle Brooks, what would you do with them?”

Her fingers stopped tapping.

She opened her mouth.
