# Prologue: The Road to the Stars

The world was small once.

It had one sun that anyone had ever visited, and a sky that humanity could only look at. Then, within the span of a single life, the doors began to open.

The first was a door in the body. Clinics appeared in the great cities, and from them the broken walked out whole. Mothers carried in children who were meant to die and carried out children who ran. Old men who had made their peace with the end found that they had years left to spend. Officials came to close the clinics, and stood in the waiting rooms, and went home.

The second was a door between the stars. Ships arrived that crossed in days the distances that had once been measured in lifetimes, and crowds gathered on the docks to watch them leave.

The third was a door in the work of the world. Cities rose in a season. Hands that had worked for generations found that they were no longer wanted, and said so in the streets.

And no one knew who had opened them. The firms that sold these wonders had no history. No inventor was ever named, and no laboratory was ever found.

In those years, a venture to the nearest star came close to dying before it began. The money behind it vanished within a few months, all at once, as though the world had changed its mind. A stranger's gold carried it the rest of the way. In the end the ships sailed, and the whole world watched them go, and from that day the stars were a plan.

Then the sky answered.

Out past the last of the planets, a small cold moon lit up with a light that had no name. Soon after, beneath the red sands of Mars, people found the dead. A thousand of them, in armor, lying where they fell among the marks of a battle that no one living had seen. They were not human. They had fought something, and lost, and been left there for longer than anyone could say.

The moon was a door. Through it a ship could cross, in a breath, a gulf that the swiftest ships needed weeks to cover, and beyond it were more doors. Humanity went through, and found the nearest worlds empty. No one answered. No one came.

Three questions followed them through. Were they alone? Where had the dead gone? And would anything return, for a door opens in both directions?

After that the people spread like seed on the wind. Every flag and every company planted settlers as far as its ships could safely reach, and the colonies took root and grew, one after another, until a settled world lay under nearly every star within reach.

But every gift carried a price. The colonies prospered, and prosperity gave people something to quarrel over. Every charter became a prize, every cargo a temptation, every road between the stars a road that someone wanted to own. It began in courtrooms and customs houses. Then the same ships that carried settlers began to carry soldiers, for hired guns were cheap and the stars were wide and no one could say whose law ran out there. Raiders followed the cargo. Fleets followed the raiders. Some of the soldiers were never paid. Some were never found.

There was a voice that claimed to rule them all, but it never had the power to back such an assertion. In time its delegates were murdered, and the world held one of the great powers to blame. The voice cried out in the only way it could, by telling the world that it was helpless to win justice for its slain. The great powers heard a confession and took it as a license, and each turned on all the others.

Within the hour, a man stepped forward. He was the master of the colonial league, and he said that where killers go unpunished the sword is the only law left. He would take the burden of justice upon himself, on behalf of all humankind, and teach the world again what justice and the rule of law were for. Before the day was out he had proclaimed the Imperium of Sol, and taken the name of Emperor.

So began the war that had no sides. The Emperor's demand was one law, and for every power a choice: concede and face justice, or be crushed. Some conceded, believing in the cause. Many more refused. There was never an Earth against the colonies. There was every nation for itself, fighting the league and one another at once, each racing to rebuild before the rest, so that it could rule them when it did. Treaties were signed at dawn and broken by dusk. The league was the smallest party of all, with few friends and many enemies, and for ten years no one stood against it together. Then the survivors, who could no longer deny that they were losing, joined in a single alliance and fought on for five years more.

In the end the fleets were ash and the treasuries were dust, and the league stood in the ruins, because its factories and shipyards had never stopped. The last bastion of the alliance fell, and the alliance conceded. There was no amnesty. Help to rebuild went only to those who had stood aside from the war. Every power, whether it had bent or been broken, was folded into the Imperium under one law, and those who still refused were found in the night, and their leaders were gone.

Then the Emperor spoke of the doors. They were not made by human hands, he said, nor by any hand that anyone could name. No one knew what they were, or how far a door might reach, or what would follow if one were struck and broken. So the doors were shut to every traveler. One by one the roads between the stars were lifted out of the systems that held them and carried to the dark beyond the Imperium's lights, and laid to rest there behind a wall of guns and mines, with a single narrow gate left open to those the Emperor allowed. Earth has had no road to the stars since. Only ships.

Now the lights of eleven thousand worlds burn under the Emperor's law. Most have no one living on them at all, and the machines keep the watch. Fifty billion people live within that light, perhaps ninety. On the quiet worlds, soldiers still drill every day, for every trouble their officers can imagine.

Beyond that light, a hundred and sixty thousand more stars wait in the dark.

This is the story of how that came to pass, and of the ones who lived through it.
