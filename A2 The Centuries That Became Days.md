# The Centuries That Became Days

*“Then, in the 2060s, ships appeared that could cross to another star in days. The papers of the time give their pace as about a light year a day. Alpha Centauri lies a little over four light years from Sol, so the trip took four days or so. Until that decade no human being had gone beyond the Solar System, and Alpha Centauri had been, for every earlier generation, a destination fit only for the dreams of grandchildren’s grandchildren. The chronicles show crowds at the first departures, and governments scrambling to understand what had been placed in their hands.”*

— *The Road to the Stars*

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A2 · Maya Brooks (25)*

### *Rotterdam, 2063*

Three forms lay stacked on Maya Brooks’s desk, squared at the corners, and on each of them the same line was empty. She had noticed it at seven that morning, and by noon she had stopped pretending to read anything else. Rain ticked against the window of the brokerage office, a cover that sold marine insurance on the ground floor and sold her attention on the third, and beyond the glass the harbor cranes swung slowly through the murk.

It had been a little over a year since she walked out of a ward in Marseille on her own legs. She had not stopped counting. It was a habit she had given up trying to break, because the work rewarded it.

The work was Collection. Her desk gathered what governments wanted and what they said they wanted, and set the two side by side. This week, three ministries on three continents had asked a shipping intermediary for the same thing: a meeting with whoever sold the drive.

None of them could say what drive. A bench demonstration in a hangar outside Toulouse had been seen by eleven people, but a rumor out of a Texas test stand had been seen by none, and between the two a whole class of men in good suits had begun to run.

She had tallied the requests the way she tallied everything. Three ministries. Nine inquiries through four intermediaries, the earliest dated nineteen days ago.

Two offers of purchase, one of them already in six figures, though no one had named a price for something nobody could describe. A defense ministry that wanted to buy outright, a transport ministry that wanted to license, and an industry ministry that wanted, as far as she could tell, to be photographed beside it.

The forms were the part she could not set down. Each ministry had its own acquisition request, with its own letterhead and its own language, fifteen fields in the French, twenty-two in the German, and thirty-one in a dense English that had clearly been drafted by a committee afraid of being sued. They had been filled in by different hands: one typed, one in a tight, upright script that pressed hard through the page, one with a coffee ring over the signature block.

And on every one, a line stood empty.

The French called it *Concepteur*, the German *Urheber*, and the English *Originating party, designer of record*. Her eye kept going back to it the way a tongue goes back to a missing tooth. It was not an oversight, because oversights fall in different places on different forms. This line sat in the same spot on each, third from the bottom, left of the signature block, with a small pale rule under it where a name belonged.

Three governments, three languages, but the same blank.

She thought about what that meant, and the deduction was cold and simple. Every ministry’s engineers had gone looking for the inventor first, and every one had come back with nothing to write down. The requests said what they were willing to pay for. They did not say whom they were paying, because they did not know.

In the bottom drawer of her desk lay a single slim folder she had been handed her first week, and had never been able to close. *Galveston, 2059. No claim. No suspect.* A bomb, and no one’s name on it.

She did not take it out. She did not need to. The two things had a shape in common, and she did not like what the shape suggested.

The radiator knocked twice. Somewhere below her, a phone rang and was answered.

She pulled a clean sheet of paper toward her and uncapped her pen.

She slid the three forms out of the stack.

She laid them side by side.

Letterheads, left to right. French. German. English.

She put one finger under each line.

Blank.

Blank.

Blank.

Her pen stopped over the third form. The nib touched the paper, and a dot of ink bled outward and kept bleeding.

She could not make her hand lift it.

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A2 · Hannah Shepard (33)*

### *Aboard the test ship, 2064*

The pre-flight checklist ran to thirty-seven lines, and not one of them mentioned the drive. Hannah Shepard had read it three times on the launch pad, laminated card held flat against her knee, and the gap sat in the middle of the page like a missing stair. Brakes, hull seals, life support, field harness, comms, escort protocol.

She had written the last one herself. Between the harness and the comms, where the heart of the ship should have been, was a blank line with nothing to initial.

Her firm had been hired to guard the first crossing beyond the Solar System. Four days to Alpha Centauri, if the papers were right, at a light year a day. No human being had ever gone farther than the Moon’s long shadow and come back, and now she sat strapped into a flight couch on the bridge of a ship with six people aboard, looking at a card.

She had asked about the blank on the launch pad. The pilot, Teodor Vance, a lean man with a surgeon’s hands, had shrugged and said the supplier’s technician signed off on the drive in a sealed room, and that was the procedure. She had asked to meet the technician. Nobody could find him.

The engineer, Priya Nair, had spent a week trying to get a manual and had been handed a single-page schematic with no maker’s plate, no part numbers, and no name at the bottom. So Hannah had done what she had always done, which was to look for what was not there. The hull carried no markings.

The drive housing carried no serial. The cradle that held it had been welded by hands that left no initials. A ship built by a company that wished to be forgotten, she thought, or by one that had never had to ask to be remembered.

Launch had been gentle, almost rude in its ease. The Moon fell behind them in an hour. By the second day the Sun had shrunk to a hard white ember, and the field hummed around the hull in a single low note she could feel in her back teeth.

Vance had stopped joking sometime in the first twelve hours. Nair had stopped looking at her board and begun simply watching the viewport, her hand pressed flat against her mouth.

Past the last outpost of any human sensor, the stars ahead had drawn tight and turned a cold, hard blue. Hannah had not expected it to be beautiful. She had expected it to be a procedure. She found, a little to her own surprise, that her eyes were wet, and she let them be, because there was no one on the bridge who was not doing the same.

Midpoint came at the forty-eighth hour. Vance announced it softly, as though a loud voice might disturb the field. Two light years out in two days. No one in the history of the species had ever been so far from home.

“Nominal,” Nair said. “All lines nominal.”

The hum dropped half a tone.

Hannah’s thumbnail dug into the laminate.

The lights went red.

The viewport jerked. The stars smeared, snapped back, smeared again.

Her palm hit the bulkhead. The hum skipped under it like a missed heartbeat.

“Nair.”

“I see it.”

“Teodor, hold the heading.”

“There’s nothing to hold.” His voice cracked. “The board says we’re doing one light year a day. And zero. And three.”

All three readouts glowed on the console. All three were the same instrument.

The hum stuttered. Caught. Stuttered again.

Nair tore her restraint loose and slammed her hand against the drive panel. She read the schematic. She read it again.

“It shouldn’t do that,” she said. “There is nothing in there that can stutter.”

Hannah’s breath had stopped somewhere in the second stutter. She did not start it again.

The ship held its note, ragged and broken, but carried them on.

Nobody aboard could say why it should.

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A2 · Emil Varga*

### *The Lisbon docks, 2065*

The fare was printed on the departure board in letters a hand tall, and it was more than Emil Varga’s pension paid in two years. He had read it three times standing at the rail, and each time the number had stayed exactly where it was. *Scheduled passenger service. Sol to Alpha Centauri and return. Eight days. No landing.* Beneath it, in smaller type, the next sailing, and the one after, as if this were any ferry on any river.

He had paid twelve euros for a dockside pass, which was a fair price for a place to stand. The rail under his palm was cold and gritty with rust, and the river wind came up the Tagus smelling of diesel and wet stone. In his coat pocket a steel thermos had gone lukewarm against his hip. He was seventy-eight years old, and he had walked from the tram stop slowly, with a stick he did not like, because the crowd had begun to gather before dawn.

There were thousands of them. Families sat on suitcases, and schoolchildren were stacked on their fathers’ shoulders. A man near him was selling paper flags from a tray at three for five euros, and Emil had not bought one, though he had watched a little girl wave hers so hard that she knocked her own hat off. The air was thick with the sound of people trying not to shout early.

He had a name for what he felt, and it was not the right one. It was not excitement. He had been excited before, at the births of his children, at a goal in the last minute of a final. This was lower in the body, and slower, a pressure behind his sternum like a held breath that had been held for seventy years.

When he was nine, his father had ground a telescope lens from the bottom of a bottle and a cardboard tube from a roll of carpet. They had set it up on the roof of their building in Setúbal on a night with no moon. Through it, a smear of light had resolved into a dozen smaller smears, and his father had said, very quietly, that those were suns.

“Each one has its own,” he had said, and not finished the thought. Emil had understood that his father was telling him something about the size of the world, and that the thing was unbearable, and that it would be a long time before he was allowed to stop thinking about it.

For every generation before this one, the stars had been a thing to look at. He had grown up knowing that as a plain fact, the way he knew the sea was salt. Now the board said *Eight days*, and a young woman in a pressed uniform was walking the line of passengers with a tablet, checking names.

Sixty seats. He knew the number, because the newspapers had printed it. He knew the passengers were mostly rich, with a handful of contest winners who had cried on the evening news, and that a man from the ministry had called it “the beginning of an ordinary service.”

He had laughed at that when he read it, a short bark that made his wife look up from her crossword. She had been dead three years now. He found himself wishing, absurdly, that she were standing at the rail with him, complaining about the wind.

A bell rang somewhere high on the gantry.

The crowd quieted all at once. Even the children.

He had expected to cheer, but his throat would not make the sound.

The ship stood in its berth at the end of the long pier, a plain pale hull with no flag and no name painted on it, so clean it looked unfinished. It did not roar. A hum rose through the soles of his shoes, low and even, and the air around the hull began to shimmer like heat over a road.

Then the hull simply was not there.

It had gone upward, he understood afterward, but it had gone so fast that the eye refused to follow. There was a thin bright line against the morning cloud, and the line folded into a point, and the point was gone.

The pier stood bare.

The wind moved through the gantry. A flag fell off a railing.

Then the sound broke over him like surf.

Thousands of voices. Hats in the air. Strangers gripping one another’s arms. A teenager beside him was screaming a word that had no letters in it.

Emil’s face was wet.

He did not know when it had started. He did not know why. It was not grief, and it was not joy, and it was larger than either, the way his father’s whisper on the roof had been larger than a word.

The berth was empty.

The crowd was still cheering.

His hands were still raised.

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A2 · Colm Farrady*

### *A ministry office, 2065*

The folder in Colm Farrady’s hands weighed almost nothing, and it had taken him a week to understand that this was the problem. It was a thick, handsome thing, buff card with a red cord, the kind the ministry used for a purchase of national importance. Inside lay a sheaf of search results, and every page was stamped in the same violet ink: *no record, no record, no record.*

He set it on the desk and it settled without a sound.

The signature he needed belonged to the Treasury’s accounting officer, a gaunt woman named Pryce who had held the post for longer than anyone could remember and had never been talked past a blank line. The purchase was approved.

The minister had approved it on the day he watched a ship fold into the morning sky over Lisbon on the canteen television, with a half-eaten sandwich forgotten in his hand. The money was allocated, the quarter closed on Friday, and unspent money lapsed on the stroke of midnight. Colm had until then, and a form with one line empty.

*Originating party, designer of record.*

He had filled in every other line. He knew their order the way he knew the stairs in his own house, in the dark. This one sat third from the bottom, left of the signature block, with a small pale rule under it, and Pryce had sent the form back twice with the same note in green ink. *Who are we paying?*

The ministry’s engineers had been given the job of answering. They had been very thorough, which was the trouble. They had searched the patent offices of every major economy and a good many minor ones, but they had found no filing that touched the drive, or the field, or the housing, or anything resembling the principle by which a ship crossed four light years in four days.

They had gone to the universities next. No dissertation foreshadowed it, and no citation trail led toward it. No obscure paper from thirty years back, when a young woman in a bad lab might have written down the first idea, existed anywhere in the literature. There was no lineage.

A thing that large was supposed to have ancestors, a long untidy family of near-misses and failed prototypes, and this one had come into the world like a coin from an empty hand.

He had asked the senior engineer what that meant. The man had taken off his glasses and rubbed the bridge of his nose until the skin went red. “It means either somebody has hidden every trace of ten years’ work,” he said, “or somebody has done it in no time at all. I do not know which would be worse.”

Colm turned the folder over. The red cord had left a groove in his thumb.

He thought of the people he had watched on the canteen television, the crowd with their flags and their upturned faces, and of the old man in the front row who had been crying with his hands in the air. They had not asked who made it. They had looked up. He envied them, with a slow, dull envy, because a person who only had to look up never had to fill in a form.

A trolley rattled past in the corridor and faded.

He pulled the last unopened sheet from the bottom of the stack. A single page, thinner than the rest, forwarded that morning by a clerk in the legal department who had not known what it was. It was a corporate registry extract. It named the firm that had sold the demonstration to the three ministries, the only firm anyone had ever been able to point at, and it carried a date at the head of the page.

He had meant to skim it.

He read the date.

He read it again.

He picked up the phone. He dialed the registry. A woman answered, brisk and bored, and he gave her the firm’s name.

Keys clicked.

“Incorporated,” she said, “eleven months ago.”

He set the receiver down.

Down the corridor, a stamp came down on a folder.

Once. Again.

He could not stop hearing it.

The drive had flown for two years, but the firm that sold it was eleven months old.

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A2 · Hannah Shepard (35)*

### *Aboard the escort flagship Marlin, 2066*

The drill card in Hannah Shepard’s hand was the one she had written herself, after the stutter, in the long quiet of a hotel room in Geneva two years ago. She had laminated it in a copy shop with a stranger’s machine, and the corners had since gone soft from handling. Five steps in plain black type.

*Dead drive. Close the gap. Tether. Match field. Bring them home.* She knew it by heart now. She carried it anyway.

Her flotilla had escorted nineteen scheduled crossings since the first sightseeing run, with four ships in a loose diamond around a plain pale hull full of paying passengers. The routine had a sleepy, almost parochial feel by now. The passengers wore sweaters and complained about the coffee. The field hummed its low note, and the stars ahead drew into their cold blue lines, and the days went by at a light year apiece.

On the third day, Hannah noticed what was not happening.

Machines announced their deaths. That was a fact so old she had stopped thinking of it as knowledge. A pump coughed before it failed, a bearing sang, a gauge crept toward red, and a good officer learned to read the ship’s small complaints the way a nurse reads a pulse.

In two years of crossings she had never once heard the drive complain. It ran, and then it did not, and in between the instruments held a perfect, unbroken silence. She had written that into the drill’s margin, in pencil, where nobody would read it.

“Marlin, Halyard.”

The voice on the channel was young and perfectly steady.

“Go, Halyard.”

“Captain, I’ve lost my field.”

The hum dropped half a tone.

Hannah was already out of the chair.

“Close the gap,” she said. “Match speed. Full tether.”

Her fist was tight around the card.

The Marlin banked. Out in the viewport the Halyard hung two hundred meters off the port quarter, its field still shimmering at the edges, a pale bubble gone ragged and thin.

“Field at forty percent.”

“Thirty.”

“Tether line away!”

A bright thread of light snapped across the gap. It touched the Halyard’s hull and held.

“Match.”

The Marlin’s field swelled, wider, slower, like a hand opening. It reached out. It touched the Halyard’s edge, and the two fields met in a seam of cold blue.

“Contact. They’re inside.”

Nine crew aboard the Halyard. Her pulse beat in her teeth.

The Halyard’s lights flickered, steadied. The ship rode inside the Marlin’s field like a child inside a coat.

“Holding,” the engineer said. “We’re holding.”

She let out her breath.

The card creased in her fist. She had not noticed bending it.

Then Hannah looked at the Halyard’s drive housing.

It was glowing.

Not a failing glow. Not the slow dimming that the drill assumed, a field fading from the edge inward until the last of it winked out. The housing was brightening. A white core was gathering at the center of the hull, and the light was moving outward in a ring.

“That is not step five,” she said.

The ring reached the hull. The hull rippled.

The light collapsed inward, all at once, a ball of white folded to a point.

The point was gone.

The Halyard went with it. Hull, tether, nine crew.

The tether line hung in the field, cut clean, its end still glowing. Then dark.

“Halyard,” the engineer said. “Halyard, Marlin.”

Nothing answered.

Hannah’s mouth was open. No sound came.

The card was bent double in her fist.

Nobody had written a card for that.
